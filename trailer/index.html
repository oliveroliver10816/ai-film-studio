Why this story

The reference film survives scrutiny because it avoids scrutiny — five lines of dialogue in 88 seconds, weak shots framed into silhouette, shallow focus hiding everything but one face, and a flash montage where nothing has to hold up. This script is built to exploit exactly those conditions.

Two faces only. Four spoken lines. Every wide shot is dark or backlit. Every close-up is one face on a plain field. No crowds, no hands doing fine work, no readable text on screen — the four things that still betray generated video.

Nothing rides on this commercially. It is the test piece: it proves a face survives ten shots, a voice matches a mouth, and a cut reads as film. Everything learned here transfers to work that does earn.

The ten shots

Each shot has two prompts. The still prompt goes to Flow to make the keyframe. The motion prompt goes to Wan on your machine, which animates that keyframe. Build the shot in the keyframe — wardrobe, set and light are decided there, not argued with later.

The flash montage

Fourteen cuts in 4.10 seconds, 0.293 s each. At this length nothing has to survive inspection, which is the point: this is where the impossible shots go. Each is a single gesture, no faces held longer than a beat.

#InImageSource

Cheapest way to make these: take the last frame of a sustained shot you already rendered, push it through one second of motion, and use a fraction of it. Fourteen fresh generations is the expensive way to do the least important 4 seconds of the film.

The four lines

Length is set by pauses, not by word count. Every sentence boundary costs roughly nine tenths of a second. If a line comes back too long for its shot, merge the sentences rather than cutting words, and re-measure — the same text does not produce the same duration twice.

Send the files as WAV if the account allows it, MP3 at 192 kbps or better otherwise. Do not trim the silence at the start or end.

Order of work

  1. Two master portraits in Flow — Elena and Adrian, prompts above. Upscale and download. Nothing else starts until both faces are approved. Bob
  2. Face-survival test. I take the approved portraits and generate four stills each across different rooms and lighting. If a face drifts, we fix it here — a still costs seconds, a video costs minutes. Claude
  3. Voice audition — one line, two or three candidate voices per character, sent back for a pick. Runs in parallel with steps 1–2. VA
  4. Ten keyframes in Flow, one per sustained shot, each built from the approved portrait. Bob
  5. Ten shots rendered on the Blackwell, three takes each, logged to the render ledger with their own GPU seconds and keep rate. About 49 minutes at three takes. Claude
  6. Four final voice files in the chosen voices, once the pick is made. VA
  7. Montage, cut, grade and master. Grade every shot to one master reference, never to the shot before it — matching neighbour to neighbour is how a film drifts colour across a minute. Claude

Steps 1, 3 and 4 are the only things not on our machine. Everything else runs over the bridge and reports itself. If the two portraits and the voice pick arrive, the rest is a day's work with no further input needed.
