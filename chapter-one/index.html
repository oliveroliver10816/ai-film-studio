How to run it

Dola · Seedance 2.0 fast · free accounts

Every shot is two prompts: one that makes the still, one that makes the still move and speak. Do them in that order, one shot at a time, and keep the still even when the video fails — the still is the expensive part and it is reusable.

The loop, per shot

  1. Generate the keyframe from the IMAGE PROMPT. Either route works: Google Flow on the other PC, or Qwen-Image-Edit 2511 on the Blackwell conditioned on the locked cast master. The local route is the one that has already held a face across ten shots, so prefer it.
  2. Crop the watermark if it came from Flow — four-pointed star, bottom right, about 115 px in from each edge. Output 1920×1080, 16:9.
  3. Upload the keyframe to Dola as the first frame, then paste the VIDEO PROMPT.
  4. If a second reference image is allowed, add the cast master named on the card and keep the two @Image lines at the top of the prompt. If it is not, delete those two lines and everything else still works.
  5. Generate at 10 seconds, 16:9. Keep the take if the face holds and no text appears anywhere in frame. Those are the only two automatic rejects.

The four syntax marks

Straight out of ByteDance's own prompt guide. Everything else can be plain English; these four tell the model what kind of sound it is looking at.

ContentSyntaxExample
One trap. The same guide also uses angle brackets for character placeholders — <Character A> — in its multi-reference templates. That collides with the sound-effect mark. Every prompt below therefore writes character names in plain capitals and reserves < > for sound effects only. Do not mix the two conventions in one prompt.

Five rules that are in every prompt on this page

  1. No readable text, ever. Four of these twelve shots involve a contract. Legible paper is one of the last reliable tells of generated video, so the folder stays shut, pages sit at forty degrees to the lens, and the one thing the audience needs to know is read aloud in shot nine instead of shown.
  2. No gesturing. She moves her head and eyes only; he is slow to turn and never rushed. Hands are the other tell, so hands stay on the table or at their sides in all twelve shots.
  3. The camera is locked in eleven shots of twelve. One slow push in shot nine, one handheld drift in shot eleven. Restraint everywhere else is what makes those two land — and a locked camera is also the cheapest way to stop a generator inventing motion it cannot sustain.
  4. The identity block is repeated in full, every time. The model has no memory between clips. Anything that must hold across two minutes has to be restated in all twelve prompts, which is why they are long.
  5. Compose for 2.39:1, do not render bars. Frame with headroom and crop in ffmpeg at the end. Asking the model for black bars wastes pixels and it renders them inconsistently.

Where the dialogue sits

Each column is one 10-second clip · red = a spoken line, in its time budget

The one thing that can quietly break this package is a line that does not fit inside ten seconds. Speech duration comes from pauses, not word count — roughly 0.85 s per sentence boundary on top of the words themselves — so every line is placed in a time range in the prompt and drawn here against the clip it has to live in. Two shots carry no dialogue at all, on purpose: the first and the last.

Plain text or JSON

Every shot ships in both · we settle it by measuring, not by believing

A side-by-side doing the rounds ran the same scene through the same model twice — once as a paragraph, once as JSON with labelled fields — and reports that the paragraph version dropped a gloved hand out of the scene entirely while the JSON version kept it. The fix it proposes is to give small elements their own field instead of burying them mid-sentence.

Watched it frame by frame, and the demo does not clearly show what it claims. Both halves have a hand early on and both lose it later. So treat the claim as unproven — but the technique is still right, and it is right for a reason that has nothing to do with that clip: a labelled field cannot be skimmed past. It is the same instinct as ByteDance's own guide, which drops natural language the moment things get complicated and switches to [Characters], [Props], [Scenes], [Stage 1] blocks. JSON is that idea taken all the way.

Assembly & the audio question

What happens after the twelve clips land

Read this before you generate. Music is written into all twelve prompts, as asked — so this run also tests whether Seedance's score generation is any good. But be aware of the trade: generated music is baked into the same audio track as the dialogue. Twelve clips scored independently will not match each other, and you cannot swap the music out afterwards without losing the dialogue with it. So: run it this way first and judge the result. If the score does not hold across the twelve, regenerate with the single ( ) line deleted from each prompt — the clips then come back with dialogue, ambience and effects only, and one continuous bed goes underneath in the edit. That is how it is normally done, and it is one deleted line away.

The score as written is a single idea across the whole film, which is what makes the mismatch risk visible rather than hidden: one sustained low cello note opens it (shots one and two), it drops to a single piano note under her first line, it stops completely at the signature in shot seven and stays gone through shot eight — that silence is doing the work of the turn — then a low string cluster rises from shot nine, swells under the payoff in shot eleven, and resolves back to the opening cello note as she leaves.

Cut order

  1. Concatenate the twelve accepted takes in order, no transitions — every cut in this film is a straight cut.
  2. Crop to 2.39:1 and letterbox to the delivery frame in ffmpeg.
  3. Master to −14 LUFS, true peak −1.5 dBTP. The Korean reference sits at −24, ten under platform target — do not copy that.
  4. Keep every reject. Failed takes are the measurement: keep-rate per shot is the number that tells us whether this route is usable at all.
